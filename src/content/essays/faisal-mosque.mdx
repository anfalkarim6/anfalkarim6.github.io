On a school trip when I was eleven, our teacher told us to look up as we walked into the prayer hall of the Faisal Mosque. I expected a dome, because every mosque I knew in Quetta had one. Instead the ceiling rose away from me in folds, like the inside of a huge paper lantern, and the walls disappeared into it.

I have been trying to work out how that ceiling was made ever since.

## A competition, and a risk

In 1969 Pakistan held an international competition for a national mosque in its new capital. The winner was a Turkish architect, Vedat Dalokay, with a design that surprised many people: no dome at all. Instead, an eight-sided concrete shell folds down to the ground like a single great roof, and four slender minarets in the Turkish style, almost ninety metres tall, rise at the corners of the courtyard.

Dalokay said the form came from the tents of desert nomads. To many people it was unfamiliar at first; today it is Pakistan's national mosque and one of the most recognisable buildings in the country. It was funded by Saudi Arabia with the support of King Faisal, and named in his honour after his death. Construction began in 1976 and the mosque opened in 1986.<Note n={1}>For several years after it opened it was the largest mosque in the world. That fact is on every tourist sign. What the signs don't say is that its size is hidden: from the hills it reads as a small white tent, and only up close does the scale arrive.</Note>

## Why the tent works

A dome is a centre. It gathers everything under one point. The tent does the opposite: it spreads. The roof touches the ground at its corners, so the building seems to be pegged into the earth rather than sitting on it.

Islamabad is a planned city, laid out on a grid in the 1960s, and much of it still feels new. The mosque anchors it to the landscape. It stands at the end of a long axis, right against the Margalla Hills, and the hills become its backdrop, its dome, if you like.

## Modern, and still sacred

What I admire most is that Dalokay did not decorate a modern building with old motifs, and did not copy an old building in new concrete. He began with what a mosque needs in order to serve the people who pray in it (a clear direction of prayer, a large shaded space, a courtyard, a call to prayer that carries) and answered each need with a modern structure.

The minarets carry the call. The courtyard gathers people. The folded shell makes one enormous column-free hall. The qibla wall is the calmest surface in the building.

When I sketch a building now, I try to do the same thing before I draw a single line: write down what the building *needs*, and only then what it should look like.
