In 1927 Le Corbusier published what he called the *Five Points of a New Architecture*. A few years later he finished a weekend house outside Paris for the Savoye family that demonstrates all five at once. Ninety-five years later, every architecture student still draws it. I wanted to know which points still hold up.

## The five points

1. **Pilotis.** Lift the building on thin columns and let the ground run underneath.
2. **The free plan.** If columns carry the floors, walls don't have to hold anything up, so they can go wherever the rooms need them.
3. **The free façade.** For the same reason, the outside wall can be designed freely.
4. **The ribbon window.** A long horizontal window that lights a room evenly from end to end.
5. **The roof garden.** Give back the ground the building took, on top.

## How the house uses them

At Villa Savoye the ground floor is pulled back behind the columns, and its curved glass wall follows the turning circle of a car: you were meant to drive underneath the house and be dropped at the door. A ramp then carries you slowly up through the middle of the building, from the shaded ground to the sunny roof. Le Corbusier called this the *promenade architecturale*: the house is meant to be walked, not just seen.

## What didn't age well

The house leaked from the start. Madame Savoye wrote to the architect about rain in the hall, rain on the ramp and a soaked garage wall. Flat roofs and thin walls were new technology, and the technology wasn't ready. During the war the family left; afterwards the house was nearly demolished, until it was protected as a historic monument in 1965.<Note n={1}>Le Corbusier was still alive when the French state listed it. It is rare for an architect to see their own building declared historic.</Note>

The ribbon window also has a weakness in a hot climate: a long strip of glass with no shade is a long strip of heat. In a Pakistani summer, a ribbon window facing west would make a room unbearable by four o'clock.

## The point we already had

The fifth point is the one I find funniest, because Pakistan never needed Le Corbusier to invent it. Our flat roofs are already gardens, laundries, places to dry fruit in the sun, bedrooms on summer nights and, in Lahore during Basant, launch pads for kites.

So my score: the free plan and the promenade are as powerful now as in 1931. The ribbon window needs a sunshade. And the roof garden was here all along.
