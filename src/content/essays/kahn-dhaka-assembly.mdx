The first time I saw the National Assembly in Dhaka it was not in Dhaka. It was on a laptop in my school library, in the last ten minutes of *My Architect*, the film Nathaniel Kahn made about his father. The camera drifts across a lake toward a cluster of concrete drums and blocks, cut with circles and triangles so large they look as if a giant had borrowed a set of drawing templates. People are praying on the grass. Then the camera goes inside, and the building fills with light.

I watched those ten minutes perhaps twenty times. This essay is my attempt to understand why.

## Twenty years in the making

The commission came in 1962, when Dhaka was the capital of East Pakistan and the government planned a second seat for the national legislature there. Kahn was sixty-one. Construction stopped during the war of 1971, and when it resumed, the building belonged to a new country. Kahn died in 1974 with the project unfinished; his associates completed it in 1982.

That is the first lesson of the building: a parliament is built for generations, not for a single government.

## Ruins wrapped around a room

At the centre is the assembly chamber, a tall, many-sided room under a concrete umbrella. Around it Kahn placed a ring of smaller buildings (offices, a prayer hall, lounges, dining rooms) and wrapped the whole cluster in thick outer walls.

The outer walls are the part everyone photographs. They are punched with enormous openings: full circles, half circles, triangles, slots. They are not windows. Behind each one is a gap, a light well, and then the real wall with the real windows. Kahn spoke about wrapping ruins around buildings: a sacrificial outer layer that takes the sun and the monsoon so the inner one doesn't have to.

In Bangladesh, where the sun is fierce and the rain arrives sideways, this is not a stylistic idea. It is a climate strategy. The light that finally reaches the rooms has bounced off two or three concrete surfaces first. It arrives soft and indirect, and somehow brighter for it.

## The marble lines

Up close, the concrete is striped with thin bands of white marble, roughly five feet apart. I assumed they were decoration. They are not. The concrete was poured one lift at a time, and every lift left a seam. Rather than hide the seams, Kahn filled them with marble, so the building tells you exactly how it was made.<Note n={1}>Kahn's most quoted line is an imaginary conversation: "What do you want, brick?" And the brick says, "I like an arch." The marble lines feel like the concrete's answer to the same question: show people how I was built.</Note>

I find this the most moving detail in the whole complex. Much of the concrete was carried up by hand, in baskets, by workers on foot. The stripes are a record of every day they worked.

## What it taught me

I tried to draw the plan from memory last summer and got it completely wrong. I drew a building with a front and a back. The assembly has neither. Every side is a face, and every face looks out over water.

The second time, I traced it from a published plan and noticed something photographs never show: the prayer hall is turned slightly off the building's grid, so that it faces Mecca. In a composition of near-perfect symmetry, the one thing that breaks the pattern is the direction of prayer.

It is a small rotation. It is also the whole building in one gesture: an order strong enough to hold a nation together, and humble enough to bend.

> The sun never knew how great it was until it struck the side of a building. (Louis Kahn)
