I have not been to the Barbican yet. This essay is written from plans, sections, a library book that weighs as much as a brick, and a very long video call with my cousin, who lives in London and walked me around the estate on her phone.

Even through a phone screen, it did not look like the Barbican I had seen on social media.

## The word problem

Online, "brutalist" has become a mood: grey concrete, dramatic shadows, a lonely figure, black-and-white filter. The word actually comes from *béton brut*, French for raw concrete, the phrase Le Corbusier used for concrete left as it came out of the formwork. In the 1950s British critics turned it into a movement about honesty: show the structure, show the materials, don't pretend.

Honesty is not the same as gloom. The Barbican is the proof.

## A city on a bomb site

The estate was built on part of the City of London that was flattened by bombing in 1940. Chamberlin, Powell and Bon designed a whole neighbourhood there: around two thousand flats, three residential towers that were the tallest in London when they were finished, schools, a library, an arts centre, and a lake.

Cars stay at ground level; people walk on raised "highwalks" above them. Gardens are tucked into courtyards and onto the tops of buildings. Almost every balcony has a built-in planter, and today many of them overflow.<Note n={1}>My cousin's favourite detail: the planters were designed into the balcony fronts from the start. The architects expected the concrete to be softened by plants. The "grey" Barbican is a photograph taken in winter.</Note>

## Made by hand

The concrete surfaces were *bush-hammered*: after the concrete set, workers attacked it with hammer tools to break away the smooth skin and expose the stone aggregate inside. It took an enormous amount of labour. The result is a surface that catches light like rough stone and weathers far better than smooth concrete.

So the most "industrial-looking" estate in London is, up close, almost entirely hand-finished. That surprised me more than anything else.

## What I'm taking from it

The Barbican was listed as a protected building in 2001, and many of its flats are now some of the most sought-after homes in London. It went from symbol of everything wrong with modern architecture to symbol of everything right, without changing at all.

That tells me that our opinions about buildings are often opinions about photographs. Before I judge a building now, I try to find its plan. Plans don't have filters.
