In Quetta, the top floor of our house is too hot to sit in by two on a summer afternoon and too cold to sleep in on a winter night. My grandmother remembers houses that weren't like this: thick earth walls, a courtyard, and rooms that stayed comfortable in both seasons. That memory is why I went looking for Hassan Fathy.

## A village of earth

In the 1940s the Egyptian government wanted to move the people of Gourna, a village built on top of ancient tombs near Luxor. Fathy, an architect trained in Cairo, was asked to design their new village. He decided to build it from mud brick, the oldest and cheapest building material in Egypt, made from the soil of the site itself.

To roof the houses without timber, which was scarce and expensive, he travelled south to Aswan and learned a technique from Nubian masons. They could build vaults and domes out of mud brick *without any formwork*: each course of bricks leans against the one before it, so the vault holds itself up as it grows.<Note n={1}>I tried this with sugar cubes and icing. It works for about four courses and then collapses. The Nubian masons do it with mud, in the heat, freehand. I have a lot of respect for that now.</Note>

The houses had courtyards to pull cool air down at night, thick walls that soak up the day's heat slowly, and wind catchers (*malqaf*) to scoop the breeze into the rooms. None of it needed electricity.

## And then nobody came

The villagers did not want to move. Their livelihoods were tied to the old village, and many did not want houses that looked, to them, like the houses of the poor. The project was never completed. Fathy wrote about the experience in a book he later called *Architecture for the Poor*.

It would be easy to tell this story as a genius misunderstood. I think it is more useful as a warning. Fathy was right about the material and the climate. He was less right about the people. A building can be perfectly designed for a place and still be wrong for the people who live there, if they were not part of deciding.

## Why it matters in Pakistan

After the floods of 2022, millions of mud houses in Sindh were destroyed. The easy conclusion is that mud is the problem. The architect Yasmeen Lari, who received the RIBA Royal Gold Medal in 2023, argues the opposite: that earth, lime and bamboo, improved and built *with* communities rather than for them, can make shelters that are cheap, low-carbon and safe.

That is Fathy's lesson with the missing half restored. Use the earth. Ask the people first.
