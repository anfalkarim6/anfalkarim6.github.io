Every afternoon in my grandmother's house, a patch of light comes through the carved wooden screen on the stairs and crawls across the floor in a pattern of stars. As a child I used it to tell the time. Only this year did I realise the screen was doing much more than making patterns.

## Four jobs, one screen

A *jaali* is a perforated screen, usually carved stone, set into a window or a wall. The Mughals made them into an art, most famously in white marble at the tomb of Salim Chishti in Fatehpur Sikri, where entire walls are carved into lace. In Lahore you can find them in the Fort and in old havelis in the Walled City.

They look decorative. They are, mostly, climate engineering:

- **Shade.** The thick stone between the holes blocks most of the direct sun. Light comes in, but heat and glare mostly don't.
- **Air.** Wind squeezed through many small openings speeds up, and passes over stone that has stayed cool in the shade.
- **Privacy.** From the bright outside you can't see into the dim inside, but from inside you can see out. For families who value their privacy, this matters a great deal.
- **Light.** The light that does get through is broken into small patches that soften the room and move with the day.<Note n={1}>This is why the patterns aren't random. Geometric jaalis repeat a module so the openings are spread evenly: no single big hole for the sun to blast through.</Note>

## My cardboard experiment

I wanted to test the shade claim, so I made two identical boxes from cardboard, each with a thermometer inside. One had an open window; the other had a window covered with a jaali I cut by hand from mount board, with about a third of its area open.

I left both on our roof for a week of afternoons in June. The jaali box was consistently cooler inside, by about two degrees. It is a rough experiment and cardboard is not marble. But the direction was clear, and I could feel the difference with my hand.

## Jaali after the Mughals

The idea never really went away. The British-Indian architect Laurie Baker built whole walls of brick jaali in Kerala in the twentieth century: bricks laid with gaps between them, cheap and cooling. Today you see concrete and metal screens on new buildings across South Asia, though many are just decoration stuck on the outside of an air-conditioned box.

The question I keep asking is: what would a jaali designed for *now* look like? Thinner, maybe made of terracotta, with openings calculated for Lahore's sun path. That question became the starting point for my reading-room project.
