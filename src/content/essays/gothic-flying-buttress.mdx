Most buildings hide their skeletons. A Gothic cathedral wears its skeleton on the outside, and that one decision changed what a wall could be.

## The problem with stone roofs

A stone vault is heavy, and it doesn't only push down. It pushes *outward*, trying to spread the walls apart like a person sitting on a folding chair. Older Romanesque churches dealt with this by making the walls very thick and the windows very small. They are beautiful, but dark.

Gothic builders in twelfth-century France found a different answer, in three parts:

- **The pointed arch** sends more of the load downward than a round arch does, and can be made taller or shorter without changing its width.
- **The rib vault** gathers the weight of the ceiling into a few lines of stone, so the load arrives at a few points instead of along the whole wall.
- **The flying buttress** catches the outward push at those points, high up, and carries it over the side aisles to heavy piers outside.

Once the loads travel along a skeleton of ribs, piers and buttresses, the wall between them carries almost nothing. So it can be glass.

## My balsa cathedral

I built a section of a nave from balsa wood and wire, with a paper vault. Without buttresses, the walls splayed outward as soon as I pressed down on the vault. With two small flying buttresses glued on each side, it held my phone.<Note n={1}>It did not hold my physics textbook. Neither did the real choir at Beauvais Cathedral, which collapsed in 1284 when its builders pushed the height too far. Gothic engineering was learned partly through failure.</Note>

The model made something click: the buttresses are not decoration. They are the force diagram, built in stone. You can stand outside a cathedral and read where every load is going.

## Notre-Dame

Notre-Dame in Paris was begun in 1163, and its flying buttresses are among the earliest anywhere. When fire destroyed its roof and spire in April 2019, the world watched a structure that had stood for more than eight hundred years. It reopened in December 2024, its roof rebuilt in oak by carpenters using medieval techniques.

## What it means for me

I'm drawn to buildings that are honest about how they stand up: Gothic cathedrals, Kahn's concrete, a bamboo shelter. When the structure is legible, the building teaches you as you walk through it.

That is a quality I want in my own work: you should be able to look at it and understand how it holds itself together.
